HSS Heat Treatment Guide: Vacuum Hardening and Double Tempering for Maximum Blade Life

Home › Blog › HSS Heat Treatment

Published: June 18, 2026 | By PanelKnife

High-speed steel (HSS) remains the material of choice for industrial wood chipper and flaker blades. But the performance difference between an average blade and an exceptional one often comes down to one factor: heat treatment. A properly executed heat treatment cycle can double or even triple blade service life. Here is the complete process broken down step by step.

Preheating Stage: 649–677°C

The first critical step is preheating. HSS contains high alloy content — tungsten, molybdenum, chromium, vanadium — and these elements make the steel sensitive to thermal shock. Loading cold blades directly into a furnace at 1000°C+ would create uneven expansion and micro-cracking. The preheating stage at 649–677°C allows the entire blade cross-section to reach a uniform temperature before the austenitizing ramp. For thicker blades (15mm+), a two-stage preheat is sometimes used: a first hold at 400–450°C, then the 649–677°C soak. Soak time is typically 1 minute per millimeter of thickness.

Austenitizing: 1010–1052°C

This is where the magic happens. At 1010–1052°C, the steel's crystal structure transforms to austenite, and alloy carbides begin dissolving into the matrix. Temperature control here is unforgiving. Undershoot by 20 degrees and you leave carbides undissolved, reducing wear resistance. Overshoot by 20 degrees and grain growth accelerates, creating brittle blades that chip on hard knots. Modern vacuum furnaces hold temperature within ±3°C of the setpoint. The austenitizing soak time ranges from 2 to 5 minutes depending on the specific HSS grade — T1, M2, and M42 all have slightly different requirements.

Quenching Methods

Quenching must cool the steel fast enough to trap carbon in solution but slow enough to prevent distortion. Vacuum furnaces typically use nitrogen gas quenching at 2–6 bar pressure. Salt bath quenching, though less common today, offers excellent heat transfer uniformity. Oil quenching is rarely used for HSS blades due to the fire risk and inconsistent results. The target cooling rate through the critical 800–500°C range should exceed 50°C per second for most HSS grades.

Double Tempering at 900°F+

Tempering is not optional — it is where hardness is actually finalized. During the first temper at 900°F (482°C) or higher, retained austenite begins transforming to martensite. A single temper leaves significant retained austenite behind, which can convert unpredictably in service and cause dimensional instability. The double tempering process — two full cycles with cooling to room temperature between — ensures near-complete transformation. Each cycle typically runs 2 hours. The result is a stable martensitic structure with finely dispersed alloy carbides.

Target HRC Results

After proper double tempering, expect Rockwell hardness values of 60–64 HRC for M2, 62–66 HRC for M42, and 58–62 HRC for T1. These ranges balance wear resistance with toughness. Blades that measure above 66 HRC are too brittle for chipper applications; below 58 HRC they will dull rapidly. Every PanelKnife blade undergoes HRC testing at multiple points across the cutting edge, with results recorded and traceable to the production batch.

Cryogenic Treatment Option

An optional but increasingly common step is cryogenic treatment. After quenching but before tempering, blades are cooled to approximately −185°C using liquid nitrogen. This converts nearly all remaining retained austenite to martensite before the tempering stage. Studies show cryogenic treatment can improve wear resistance by 20–40% in HSS cutting tools. However, it adds cost and is typically reserved for premium blade lines or applications processing highly abrasive materials like recycled wood with embedded grit.
